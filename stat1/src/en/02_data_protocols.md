## Russian Sign Language datasets

**Slovo** [@2023_kapitanov_slovo] contains 20,000 videos of 1,000 isolated RSL signs recorded by 194 signers, plus 400 videos of a "no event" class. We keep the "no event" videos as an additional class for training and validation, so the Slovo classifiers have 1,001 outputs; our test sets and the official test split contain no such videos. **Bukva** [@2024_kvanchiani_bukva] contains 3,757 videos of the 33 letters of the RSL manual alphabet from 155 signers; the videos are untrimmed and annotated with the start and end frames of the sign, and only the frames between the boundaries are used. Both datasets were recorded by many signers on various devices in varied conditions.

## UzSL pilot recordings

The pilot set was recorded for this study with an in-house program at 1280 × 720 pixels and 30 frames per second: 322 videos of 40 fingerspelling signs (29 letters of the Latin-script Uzbek alphabet, including the digraphs CH, SH and NG and the letters Gʻ and Oʻ, and the numerals 0–10). Participant 001 is the second author, who made three test recordings of one letter while checking the program; these recordings are kept in the training data of every fold and never tested. Participant 002 recorded 143 videos of 29 letters and participant 003 176 videos of 24 letters and 11 numerals, each in a single session in January 2026 (0.30–3.67 s per video, mean 1.24 s). The two main participants share 24 letters. All participants gave written informed consent to the recording, to the use of their data in research and to the publication of images in scientific articles.

## UzSL corpus from open video lessons

**Sources.** We selected openly posted UzSL video lessons of 11 channels (162 lessons, 15.23 h), taking only lessons presented by adults; lessons with children were excluded entirely. Ten channels yielded recordings (159 lessons, 15.12 h). The videos were processed only on a laboratory computer, are not redistributed and are used for internal research; the channels are referred to by codes C01–C11 and the presenters by codes S01–S09. Two channels were presented by the same person and were merged into one signer; the three pilot participants are S10–S12. Signer identity was established from channel metadata, not by face recognition, and the presenters of different channels were not compared with each other.

**Segmentation.** Keypoints were extracted from the whole lesson (Section 3.5). For words, a segment starts when a wrist rises above its resting level (the 80th percentile of the minimum wrist height minus 0.4 shoulder widths) and ends when both hands return; pauses shorter than 0.3 s are merged and segments shorter than 0.4 s are dropped. For fingerspelling and numerals, a segment is a hold of the dominant hand's shape (shape velocity below two hand sizes per second for at least 0.32 s) inside an active interval. The automatic segmentation produced 11,153 segments.

**Labelling by three cues.** Each segment receives a label only if three independent cues agree: the on-screen text, recognised with Tesseract OCR [@2007_smith_tesseract] (Uzbek Latin or Cyrillic models) every 0.5 s with logos, recurring captions and subtitle-like lines removed; the presenter's Uzbek speech, transcribed by the Whisper large-v3 model [@2023_radford_whisper] and aligned to the segment within ±2 s; and the lesson structure (a word repeated twice in a row, or, in fingerspelling lessons, the alignment of announcements with the alphabet). Video titles were not used. A segment is accepted when it has a label, plausible hand physics (0.4–4 s, hands visible in at least 60% of frames, rest around the boundaries) and at least two agreeing cues; the remaining segments were screened by an automated visual check of the rendered skeletons and text crops by a multimodal language model (Section 10) and rejected when unusable. After the removal of 46 re-uploaded duplicates (wrist-trajectory correlation ≥ 0.9) and 314 segments containing two or three signs, 5,671 lesson recordings remained; with the 322 pilot recordings the corpus has 5,993 recordings of 2,381 classes. Labels were normalised to Uzbek Latin script (apostrophes, Cyrillic-to-Latin transliteration, spelling variants merged to the most frequent form); Russian captions were translated with a 953-entry dictionary that has not been verified by a native speaker, and synonyms and identical letters of the Latin and Cyrillic alphabets are still distinct classes (Section 7).

**Label accuracy.** The automatic labels were verified by the second author in a blind comparison with the reference videos of the online UzSL dictionary imo-ishora.uz (Section 3.6). From 300 random evaluation recordings (30 per LOSO signer; the seed, the matching rule and the counting rule were fixed before the check), a reference of the same gloss existed for 113. The reviewer saw the corpus segment next to the reference, but not the model predictions, and answered "same sign", "different" or "unsure". The labels agreed for 111 of 113 pairs: 98.2% (95% CI [94.6; 100], bootstrap over signers); 57 of 57 pilot pairs and 54 of 56 lesson pairs. The other 187 recordings had no reference and were judged from the reviewer's own knowledge of UzSL (182 "same", 3 "different", 2 "unsure"); this count is reported for information only. Because only 56 lesson recordings had a reference, the measured accuracy applies to words that occur in the dictionary and was obtained by a single reviewer. Excluding the five recordings judged "different" changes the LOSO accuracies by at most 0.1 points, so all results below use all recordings.

**Composition and evaluation set.** Table 2 gives the composition. Almost all lesson vocabulary consists of words; fingerspelling comes mostly from the pilot participants. The evaluation set contains the classes with at least three recordings from at least two signers: 682 classes and 3,418 recordings (3,146 words, 272 letters and numerals); 405 classes have three or more signers, and the median class has four recordings. Classes recorded by a single signer (the pilot numerals, the letters H, I, Q, V and Y, and the Cyrillic alphabet of one channel) are therefore trained on but never tested.

## External sources

Two sources that took no part in corpus construction were used for validation; the final models were trained before either was downloaded. **N01** is an openly posted UzSL video dictionary with one adult presenter: 191 videos of 191 signs, labelled from the video titles (not verified). **The online dictionary imo-ishora.uz** has 1,556 reference videos of isolated signs performed by several adult presenters who do not appear in the corpus; the number of presenters is not annotated. Both sources are used only as test sets; their videos are not part of the training data of any model in this paper.

## Keypoint extraction and quality control

Keypoints were extracted with MediaPipe Holistic Landmarker 1.1.1 [@docs_mediapipe_holistic_landmarker] in video mode with all confidence thresholds set to 0.5; the same model file, identified by its SHA-256 hash, was used for all datasets. Following YouTube-ASL [@2023_uthus_youtube_asl], we keep 85 of the 553 landmarks: six pose points (shoulders, elbows, hips), the 2 × 21 hand points and 37 face points around the eyes, eyebrows, lips and face contour, including the two iris centres; only the image coordinates $x$ and $y$ are used. When a hand or the face is missing in some frames, its coordinates are linearly interpolated between the nearest detected frames and a validity mask $m$ is set to 0 for the filled frames; a part never detected in a clip is set to zero with $m = 0$. The hand detection rate, the share of frames with a detected hand among frames in which the wrist is visible, was 79.1% on Slovo (80.5% on average over signers, minimum 17.6%), 70.2% on Bukva and 94.3% on the pilot; the body pose was found in all frames of Slovo and the pilot. In the corpus, at least one hand is detected in 97.2% of frames on average (median 100%), both hands in 74.1%, and the face in 99.8%.

## Ethics and data protection

The pilot recordings contain the participants' faces and hands and are personal data; they are not shared. The lesson videos are publicly posted by their authors; they were used only for feature extraction and internal evaluation, are not redistributed, and the extracted keypoints, which allow partial reconstruction of the signer, are not released. # Evaluation protocols

## Signer-disjoint five-fold protocol for RSL (SD-5)

All comparisons on Slovo and Bukva use five folds with disjoint signers (Fig. 2a). On Slovo, six signers contribute at least 500 videos each and, by a rule fixed before training, are always assigned to the training data; the remaining 188 signers are divided into five groups stratified by the number of recordings, with a fixed seed. In fold $k$, group $k$ is the test set, 10% of the signers of the other groups form the validation set and the rest are used for training, so that each of the 188 signers (6,874 recordings) is tested exactly once. On Bukva, all 155 signers are divided in the same way. Before each run the code asserts that no signer and no video is shared between training, validation and test data and that all keypoint files were produced by the same landmarker model. Each configuration is trained with three random seeds on each fold.

## Leave-one-signer-out protocol for the UzSL corpus (LOSO)

Each of the ten signers with at least 20 evaluation recordings forms one test fold; the two remaining signers (14 recordings) form an extra fold that is not included in averages. All other recordings of the corpus, including single-recording classes, are used for training, so the classification head of a fold has 682 classes when training is restricted to the evaluation set and 2,033–2,378 classes when all recordings are used. There is no validation set: training budgets are fixed in advance (Section 5.4) and the weights of the last step are evaluated. Each condition is run with three seeds, giving 33 runs per model. Because every lesson presenter has one studio and one camera, LOSO measures a new person together with new recording conditions.

## Metrics and decision rules

The primary metric is top-1 accuracy. On SD-5 it is pooled over the test recordings of the five folds and averaged over seeds; 95% confidence intervals are obtained by bootstrap resampling of test signers (2,000 replicates) [@2016_efron_hastie_casi]. On LOSO it is the macro-average over the ten test signers (each signer's accuracy averaged over seeds), with intervals from 10,000 bootstrap replicates over signers; the micro-average over recordings is also given. We report top-5 accuracy, the macro-average over signers and the accuracy of the worst test signer with at least ten recordings on SD-5, and, for all datasets, macro-averaged precision, recall and $F_1$ over the classes present in the test data. A model is called better or worse than another only if the 95% confidence interval of the paired difference, computed over the same recordings, seeds and resampled signers, excludes zero; otherwise we report that no difference was established. On the pilot data, where each fold has one test participant, a difference is reported only if its sign is the same in all six seed–fold pairs. All decision rules, fold assignments and training budgets were fixed before the corresponding runs.
