# Results

All accuracies are top-1 percentages unless stated; brackets give 95% confidence intervals over signers; $\Delta$ denotes a paired difference. Every number in this section was computed from the per-recording predictions of the runs by one script released with the code.

## Keypoint models on Russian Sign Language

Table 3 compares the models under SD-5, and Fig. 4 relates accuracy to computation. MPE-A1 reaches 70.8% on Slovo and 93.2% on Bukva. It is more accurate than the keypoint Transformer by 4.4 points on Slovo and 8.1 points on Bukva, and the simple baselines trail by 8.6–42.2 points. The released ST-GCN and SL-GCN, trained with the same recipe and window, are more accurate on Slovo: 73.1% and 74.4%, $\Delta$ = +2.3 [+1.3; +3.4] and +3.7 [+2.5; +4.8] over MPE-A1, while needing 2.6 and 2.9 times more operations per window. On Bukva, SL-GCN with its authors' recipe is as accurate as MPE-A1 (93.1% versus 93.2%, $\Delta$ = −0.1 [−1.0; +0.6]) at 12.8 times more operations per clip, and ST-GCN with its authors' recipe is much weaker (68.8%). Macro-averaged precision, recall and $F_1$ over classes lie 2–3 points below top-1 accuracy on Slovo and within one point of it on Bukva, and the ranking of the models by $F_1$ is the same as by top-1 accuracy. The macro-average over signers is up to 4 points lower than the pooled accuracy on Slovo, and the worst test signer with at least ten recordings reaches only 27% for MPE-A1 (71% on Bukva), so all models leave some signers far behind.

**Re-implemented baselines understate the released code.** We first re-implemented ST-GCN and SL-GCN on the 27-node graph and the input features of MPE. With the shared recipe they reached 69.0% and 68.1% on Slovo, 4.1 [+3.1; +5.1] and 6.4 [+5.3; +7.4] points below the released code trained with the same recipe, and on Bukva our SL-GCN reached 84.9%, 8.1 points below the released code with its authors' recipe. The re-implementations simplified the original blocks (for SL-GCN the decoupled graph convolution, the spatial–temporal–channel attention and DropGraph; for ST-GCN the neighbourhood normalisation, input channels and dropout) and their initialisation. Had we compared MPE only with these re-implementations, it would have appeared 1.8–2.7 points *better* than the graph networks instead of 2.3–3.7 points worse. The recipe does not explain the strength of SL-GCN: with its authors' recipe it reaches 74.6%, the same as with the shared recipe ($\Delta$ = +0.2 [−1.0; +1.3]), and MPE-A1 trained with the SL-GCN recipe and 150-frame windows gains nothing (71.2%, +0.4 [−0.5; +1.3]) and stays 3.5 points below SL-GCN under that recipe, lower on all five folds. The keypoint Transformer, in contrast, gained 3.9 points [+2.7; +5.3] from its own recipe, which is why it is reported with it. The released ST-GCN with its repository configuration is much weaker (61.6% on Slovo, 68.8% on Bukva); we did not separate the possible causes (a short schedule, input without hand normalisation).

**Cost.** In floating-point operations MPE-A1 is cheaper than both graph networks, but on a desktop CPU (Intel Core i9-14900KF, float32, batch 1, median of 100 runs after 10 warm-up runs, eager PyTorch) it is not faster than ST-GCN: 8.4–9.3 ms against 5.7 ms, whereas the released SL-GCN needs 23.0 ms. We attribute this to the many small operations of MPE (two streams, part tokens, Conformer), which an eager run executes less efficiently than a few large convolutions. Exported to ONNX and run with ONNX Runtime 1.31 on the same CPU (trained weights, one thread, median of 100 runs; measured while the machine was partly loaded by training jobs, so an upper bound), the order changes: MPE-A1 takes 10.8 ms, the released ST-GCN 15.0 ms and the released SL-GCN 25.6 ms, and MPE-A2 1.8 ms (7.2, 7.6, 19.3 and 1.0 ms with four threads). Latencies of the same model differed by 1–2 ms between sessions and are compared only by order of magnitude; Section 6.7 reports the exported models.

## What drives accuracy

Fig. 5 shows the ablations of MPE-A1 (three seeds × five folds, paired differences). Replacing the four part tokens with a single token per frame lowers accuracy by 2.7 points [−3.3; −2.0] on Slovo and 5.7 points [−6.8; −4.7] on Bukva, the only change that hurts on both datasets. Replacing the graph convolutions with a per-part MLP (MPE-A2) leaves Slovo accuracy unchanged (+0.0 [−0.7; +0.7]) and improves Bukva by 1.3 points [+0.8; +1.8] while cutting computation 3.9 times. Removing the long-range edges costs 0.9 points on Slovo but gains 0.6 on Bukva; the graph without them is worse than no graph at all. Weight averaging adds 1.5 points on Slovo (not established on Bukva), and the three-times larger encoder adds 2.1 points [+1.6; +2.7] on Slovo but nothing measurable on Bukva. A wider sweep of input and architecture variants on Slovo (one seed each, relative to MPE-A0; full table in the Supplementary Material) points in the same direction: what matters is the information given to the model, not its topology. Removing the normalisation of Section 5.1 costs 5.0 points [−6.3; −3.7], using the hands alone 8.4 points [−9.6; −7.2] and removing the body points 2.5 points [−3.5; −1.7], whereas removing the face points (−0.5 [−1.1; +0.3]), adding depth (+0.3 [−0.6; +1.2]) or joint angles (+0.2 [−0.6; +0.9]), per-frame normalisation (−0.6 [−1.3; +0.2]) and changing $D$, $N$ or $T$ within a factor of two (−0.6 … +0.6) change nothing that the data can establish; a Transformer in place of the Conformer costs 1.5 points [−2.2; −0.6] and mean pooling in place of attention pooling 2.2 points [−3.3; −1.2].

{{FIG:fig6_ablations}}

The part tokens are not a universal gain. When our 27-keypoint input with its two coordinate frames and mask is given to the released ST-GCN and SL-GCN in place of their three raw channels, accuracy on Slovo falls by 1.3 and 3.0 points (one seed, 99% CIs exclude zero) and does not change on Bukva, so the representation does not transfer into the graph networks as "a better input". Conversely, inserting part tokens and a Conformer on top of the SL-GCN blocks gives +1.7 points [+0.6; +3.0] on Bukva but no established change on Slovo (−1.3 [−2.6; +0.1], 99% CI) ⟦numbers from the M12 report; to be recomputed from the exported predictions⟧. On the ASL dataset of the Google Isolated Sign Language Recognition competition [@2023_kaggle_gislr] (94,477 recordings, 250 classes, 21 signers), under signer-disjoint five-fold cross-validation MPE-A1 reaches 63.9% macro top-1, and removing the part tokens changes it by +0.1 [−0.3; +0.6] (99% CI over signers) ⟦from the M11-B report; to be recomputed⟧. The tokens therefore help on the two RSL datasets, where the hands are the main cue, and are neutral on a large ASL dataset with a strong model trained from scratch.

## Official Slovo split

Table 4 reports the official split, on which 3,448 of the 5,100 test videos (67.6%) come from 92 signers who also appear in training. MPE-A1 reaches 64.1% [61.4; 66.9], which matches the 64.09% published for the MViTv2-S video model [@2023_kapitanov_slovo]. Run with its released inference code, which loops short videos to fill the input window, MViTv2-S reaches only 51.4% [48.5; 54.4]; padding short videos with their last frame reproduces the published figure (64.3% [61.7; 67.1]). We had selected this setting on 966 test videos; on the other 4,134 it gives 64.15%, so the reproduction is not an artefact of that selection. Against the stronger setting the paired difference to MPE-A1 is −0.2 points [−2.2; +2.0], so no difference in top-1 accuracy is established, while MViTv2-S has the higher top-5 accuracy (90.5% versus 86.6%). The released MViTv2-S has 35.0 M parameters (140.8 MB as ONNX) and processes RGB video; MPE-A1 has 3.38 M (13.5 MB) and works on keypoints. The larger MPE reaches 67.0%.

## Uzbek Sign Language corpus: unseen signers

Table 5 gives the LOSO results and Fig. 6 the accuracy per held-out signer. Pretrained on Slovo and Bukva and fine-tuned on the 682-class evaluation set, MPE-A1 reaches 32.0% [25.0; 39.2] top-1 and 52.0% [44.3; 59.9] top-5 on a new signer (micro-averages 25.2% and 44.3%; macro-$F_1$ 20.5%). Trained from scratch with the same data and a seven times longer budget, it reaches 17.3% [12.2; 23.6]: pretraining adds 14.7 points [+11.3; +18.6] (99% CI [+10.3; +19.8]) and helps every one of the ten signers. When all 5,993 recordings and the 2,381-class head are used for training, the gain of pretraining is 12.2 points [+7.2; +17.3] (nine of ten signers), but accuracy on the evaluation classes falls from 32.0% to 26.2% (−5.8 [−8.0; −3.9], ten of ten signers): the additional single-recording classes add about 1,700 distractors to the head, among them synonyms and alternative spellings (*shapka* → *telpak*, *sosiska* → *sasiska*, Latin letters predicted as their Cyrillic counterparts), and, in an exploratory check that was not pre-registered, restricting the answer to the evaluation classes recovers all but 1.1 points. Pretraining helps words more than fingerspelling: +15.0 points [+10.8; +19.6] for the eight signers with words (all eight improve) and +11.7 points [+8.3; +16.7] for the three signers with at least ten fingerspelling recordings.

The released SL-GCN, pretrained and fine-tuned under exactly the same protocol, reaches 34.9% [26.9; 43.0]: 2.9 points [+1.5; +4.5] above MPE-A1 (99% CI [+1.3; +5.1]), higher for nine of ten signers, which repeats the ranking observed on Slovo (SL-GCN was not trained from scratch on the corpus; on the pilot it is much weaker from scratch than MPE-A1, Section 6.6). Accuracy varies widely between signers for both models, from 17% to 51% for MPE-A1; the two pilot participants, whose recordings are clean fingerspelling, are the easiest, and among the lesson presenters accuracy ranges from 17% (S03) to 43% (S08) and is not explained by vocabulary size alone. The most frequent confusions after pretraining are between letters and words of similar hand shape (*Gʻ* → *G*, *E* → *may*, *U* → *qayerga*) and between near-synonyms (*blinchik* → *quymoq*, *likopcha* → *tarelka*, *ona* → *ayol*); a full list is given in the Supplementary Material.

## External sources

Table 6 reports the final models, trained once with seed 0 on the whole corpus, on the two external sources without any adaptation. On the 93 N01 videos whose sign belongs to the 682 evaluation classes, the pretrained model reaches 33.3% [23.7; 43.0] top-1 and 53.8% top-5 (intervals over recordings, since the source has one presenter); on the 204 dictionary videos of 186 evaluation classes it reaches 33.8% [26.7; 40.1] and 60.3% (intervals over words). The LOSO estimate of 32.0% is thus reproduced on two independent sources with new people, new cameras and labels that we did not produce. The benefit of pretraining is also reproduced: with the 2,381-class head, pretraining raises top-1 accuracy from 9.3% to 24.0% on N01 (+14.7 [+6.7; +22.7] over 150 videos) and from 6.6% to 15.6% on the dictionary (+9.1 [+5.5; +12.9] over 370 words). Adding the ASL dataset to the pretraining sources gives 39.7% on the dictionary, +5.4 points [−1.1; +11.8] over Slovo and Bukva alone, and no change on N01, so a third source language is not established as helpful.

## Pilot recordings: budgets fixed in advance and within-signer optimism

The pilot set repeats the transfer experiment at a controlled scale, with two test participants and training budgets fixed before the first run (Table 7, Fig. 7b). Trained from scratch, MPE-A1 reaches 9.5%, 11.7% and 24.7% with one example per class, two examples per class and all recordings (pooled over the two test participants). Every pretrained condition is better than training from scratch at the same step in all six seed–fold pairs, 18 of 18 comparisons; the best pooled accuracies, with Slovo and Bukva together and full fine-tuning, are 40.3%, 41.2% and 47.5%. Because some test classes do not occur in the training data of a fold, these accuracies are bounded by 82.5% for participant 002 and 67.0% for participant 003. The linear probe is close to full fine-tuning (45.4% against 47.5%), the best source differs between the participants, and the graph-free MPE-A2 transfers as well as MPE-A1 (47.9% with the probe; the sign of its difference is not constant). The released SL-GCN, transferred under the same protocol, is slightly less accurate than MPE-A1 after full fine-tuning (45.9%, lower in all six pairs) and more accurate as a frozen encoder (49.8% against 45.4%, higher in five pairs with one tie, so not established by the rule). The confusion matrix of the pilot letters (Fig. 9b) shows that the errors are concentrated in the letters that are visually close in UzSL fingerspelling (*Gʻ* → *G*, *CH* → *A*/*J*, *E* → *X*/*Y*) and in the numerals, which occur in the recordings of one participant only.

A simulation on Bukva under SD-5 shows the same effect with many test signers (Fig. 7a): pretraining on Slovo raises accuracy from 10.9% to 70.7% with one training recording per class (+59.7 [+57.9; +61.5]), from 26.3% to 81.8% with three and from 63.7% to 86.8% with ten, whereas with all Bukva training data it is slightly harmful (90.9% against 92.9%, −2.0 [−2.8; −1.2]); the benefit of pretraining shrinks and can reverse when enough target data exist.

**Validation on the training participants overestimates accuracy.** Before the budgets were fixed, our first pilot experiments selected checkpoints on held-out recordings of the training participants. Fig. 8 compares, for the 23 conditions of these experiments, the validation accuracy at the selected checkpoint with the accuracy on the unseen participant: validation accuracy ranged from 54% to 100%, test accuracy from 7% to 51%, and the gap had a median of 45.2 points (range 35.0–75.1), largest for training from scratch. A model that has seen a participant is nearly perfect on new recordings of that participant and loses half or more of its accuracy on a new one. This is why all pilot and corpus results use budgets fixed in advance and the last checkpoint, and why accuracies obtained with signer overlap, including those reported for UzSL so far, should be read as upper bounds.

## Robustness and deployment

Table 8 reports accuracy when the dominant hand is removed from a share $p$ of the frames and when Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ (as a fraction of the frame width) is added to the keypoints, for the SD-5 checkpoints without retraining. Removing the dominant hand from a quarter of the frames does not change MPE-A1 (a hand is dropped in 20–25% of the frames during training); removing it from half of the frames costs 1.5 points on Slovo and 1.0 on Bukva. Noise is the stronger factor: $\sigma$ = 0.02 costs 6.4 points on Slovo and 4.2 on Bukva. MPE-A2 is more robust to noise on both datasets (+2.4 and +3.4 points at $\sigma$ = 0.02) and slightly more sensitive to missing hands on Slovo.
