::: abstract
Automatic recognition of Uzbek Sign Language (UzSL) has so far been reported on closed data and on signers who also appear in training. We present the first evaluation of UzSL recognition on unseen signers and show which factors matter when a sign language has almost no labelled data. We build a multi-signer UzSL corpus of isolated signs from openly posted video lessons (5,993 recordings, 2,381 classes, 12 signers), label it by the agreement of three independent cues and verify the labels against a video dictionary (98.2% accuracy on 113 reference pairs). On this corpus, under leave-one-signer-out evaluation over ten signers, a compact keypoint encoder reaches 32.0% top-1 and 52.0% top-5 accuracy on 682 classes; pretraining on two public Russian Sign Language (RSL) datasets, Slovo and Bukva, raises top-1 accuracy by 14.7 points with a gain for every signer, and the gain is confirmed on two external sources that took no part in corpus construction. On the RSL datasets, under a five-fold signer-disjoint protocol with paired bootstrap tests, the released code of two graph convolutional networks is 2.3–3.7 points more accurate than the encoder on Slovo while needing 2.6–2.9 times more operations, whereas our own re-implementations of the same networks understate them by 4–9 points. Ablations show that body-part tokens carry the encoder's accuracy and that its skeletal graph can be removed, reducing computation 3.9 times. On a pilot set recorded for this study, validation on recordings of the training participants overestimates accuracy on a new participant by a median of 45 points. For low-resource sign languages, signer-disjoint evaluation, cross-lingual pretraining and baselines run from released code matter more than architectural novelty.

# Introduction

Isolated sign language recognition (ISLR), in which each video contains one sign, is the building block of sign dictionaries with video search, learning applications and the vocabulary components of translation systems. For a few sign languages, progress has been driven by large public datasets: WLASL [@2020_li_wlasl], MS-ASL [@2019_joze_msasl], AUTSL [@2020_sincan_autsl] and ASL Citizen [@2023_desai_asl_citizen], and, for Russian Sign Language (RSL), Slovo [@2023_kapitanov_slovo], Bukva [@2024_kvanchiani_bukva] and Logos [@2025_ovodov_logos]. Most sign languages have no such resource: a recent survey of sign language datasets lists no dataset for Uzbek Sign Language (UzSL) [@2026_ni_sign_language_datasets_survey].

Published work on automatic UzSL recognition is recent and small. It covers fingerspelling with hand landmarks or image detectors [@2026_jovlieva_webcam_uzbek_fingerspelling; @2024_kayumov_uzsl_latin_angular_signs; @2024_akhmedov_uzsl_dactyl_yolo] and isolated dynamic signs recorded from a few signers [@2026_ergashev_cnnlstm_vs_squeezeformer_uzsl; @2025_akkomron_uzslr_isolated_dynamic]. The data are not public, and the reported accuracies (87–99%) were measured on signers who also appear in training; the authors of the largest study state that their evaluation was not signer-independent [@2026_ergashev_cnnlstm_vs_squeezeformer_uzsl]. The same problem is not limited to small in-house datasets: in the official split of Slovo, 92 of the 174 test signers also appear in the training set, and their videos make up 67.6% of the test set, and a recent study of sign language translation found that signer-dependent splits overestimate BLEU scores several-fold [@2026_artiaga_signer_dependence_slt]. A model tested on people it has seen measures memorisation of individual signing styles as much as recognition of signs.

Three obstacles therefore stand between the existing studies and a recogniser that a Deaf user in Uzbekistan could rely on: evaluation (no result on an unseen signer), data (a few hundred recordings cannot train a recogniser from scratch, and the obvious remedy, pretraining on a related and better-resourced sign language, has not been examined for UzSL) and deployment (video networks such as MViTv2 [@2022_li_mvitv2] are accurate but costly, whereas on-device keypoint estimators such as MediaPipe [@2020_zhang_mediapipe_hands; @docs_mediapipe_holistic_landmarker] turn an ordinary camera into a low-cost body and hand sensor). The sign languages of the former Soviet republics are reported to be closely related to RSL because of the centralised language policy of the Soviet period; for Kazakh-Russian Sign Language this relation has been described by corpus developers, who also note that no formal comparison with RSL exists [@2020_imashev_krsl_dataset; @2024_imashev_krsl_retrospective]. We are not aware of a formal comparison between UzSL and RSL either, so the relation motivates, but does not justify, the use of RSL data; in this work it is a hypothesis tested by transfer experiments.

This paper addresses the three obstacles with one evaluation discipline: no model is ever tested on a signer seen in training, every comparison is decided by a pre-registered rule, and every baseline that has released code is run from that code. Its contributions are:

1. **The first signer-independent evaluation of UzSL recognition.** We build a corpus of isolated UzSL signs from openly posted video lessons and a pilot recording session (5,993 recordings, 2,381 classes, 12 signers), label the lesson segments by the agreement of three independent cues, verify the labels against a video dictionary (98.2% on 113 reference pairs) and evaluate with leave-one-signer-out (LOSO) folds over ten signers. A compact keypoint encoder reaches 32.0% top-1 and 52.0% top-5 accuracy on 682 classes; the estimate is reproduced on two external sources (33.3% and 33.7%).
2. **Cross-lingual pretraining as the main lever.** Pretraining on Slovo and Bukva raises LOSO top-1 accuracy from 17.3% to 32.0% (+14.7 points, 95% confidence interval (CI) [+11.2; +18.6], a gain for 10 of 10 signers), raises accuracy on an external dictionary of UzSL from 6.7% to 15.9% and, on the pilot data with training budgets fixed in advance, from 24.7% to 47.5% (18 of 18 seed–fold pairs). A simulation on Bukva shows the same effect at a controlled scale.
3. **A fair comparison of keypoint models on RSL.** Under a five-fold signer-disjoint protocol with paired bootstrap tests over signers, a compact Multilingual Pose Encoder (MPE; 3.38 M parameters, 0.76 GFLOPs per 32-frame window) is more accurate than a keypoint Transformer and simpler baselines, but the released ST-GCN [@2018_yan_stgcn] and SL-GCN [@2021_jiang_sam_slr] are 2.3 and 3.7 points more accurate on Slovo at 2.6 and 2.9 times more operations; our own re-implementations of the two networks understate them by 4.1 and 6.4 points on Slovo and by 8.7 and 4.7 points on Bukva. Ablations show that body-part tokens, not the skeletal graph, carry the encoder's accuracy, and that the graph can be removed to save 3.9 times the computation.
4. **A measurement of within-signer optimism.** On the pilot data, selecting checkpoints on held-out recordings of the training participants overestimates accuracy on a new participant by a median of 45.2 points; on the official Slovo split, two thirds of the test videos come from training signers.

The fold lists, configurations and code are released at https://github.com/M1rsaiD/mpe-slovo-bukva ⟦tag of the final version to be set at submission⟧. The lesson videos and the pilot recordings contain faces and are not redistributed (Section 3.6).

# Related work

**Pose-based ISLR.** Keypoint-based recognisers process sequences of body, hand and face landmarks instead of pixels, which removes most appearance variation and makes the same model usable on keypoints from different devices. ST-GCN [@2018_yan_stgcn] models the skeleton as a graph and introduced the partitioning of a node's neighbourhood into root, centripetal and centrifugal subsets, which we also use; SL-GCN from the SAM-SLR framework [@2021_jiang_sam_slr] applies graph convolutions to a whole-body graph that includes both hands. Later work replaced or complemented graph convolutions with Transformers [@2017_vaswani_attention], added self-supervised pretraining of hand-aware pose models such as SignBERT+ [@2023_hu_signbert_plus], used gloss semantics [@2023_zuo_nla_slr] and applied Conformer blocks [@2020_gulati_conformer] to signer-invariant continuous recognition [@2025_isharah_signer_invariant_conformer]. Our encoder belongs to the same family but is built so that each of its components can be removed and tested under a strict signer-independent protocol.

**Pretraining and transfer across sign languages.** OpenHands [@2022_selvaraj_openhands] released pose-based models for several sign languages and studied cross-lingual pretraining; SHuBERT [@2025_gueuwou_shubert] and Uni-Sign [@2025_li_uni_sign] learn representations from hundreds of hours of video; Logos [@2025_ovodov_logos] is a large RSL dataset whose pretrained encoder transfers to other sign languages. Cross-dataset transfer has also been studied for pairs of under-resourced datasets [@2024_kindiroglu_cross_dataset_transfer] and with visual information [@2023_ryumin_cross_language_transfer]. These studies transfer between large datasets or rely on hundreds of hours of video. We examine the opposite end: transfer from two public RSL datasets to a few thousand recordings of a closely related language, with an explicit rule for fixing training budgets when no validation signers of the target language exist.

**Datasets and evaluation protocols.** AUTSL [@2020_sincan_autsl], the ChaLearn LAP 2021 challenge built on it [@2021_sincan_chalearn_lap_islr], MS-ASL [@2019_joze_msasl], ASL Citizen [@2023_desai_asl_citizen] and FluentSigners-50 [@2022_mukushev_fluentsigners50] evaluate on signers who do not appear in training. For RSL, Slovo [@2023_kapitanov_slovo] contains 20,000 videos of 1,000 signs from 194 signers and Bukva [@2024_kvanchiani_bukva] 3,757 videos of the 33 letters of the RSL manual alphabet from 155 signers; the official Slovo split is not fully signer-disjoint, which makes it suitable for comparison with published results but not for measuring generalisation to new signers. YouTube-ASL [@2023_uthus_youtube_asl] proposed a compact subset of 85 MediaPipe landmarks that we adopt as model input.

**UzSL recognition.** We reviewed the UzSL literature up to 2026 in [@2026_onarkulov_slr_review_uzsl]. One system recognises Uzbek fingerspelling from a webcam with MediaPipe Hands, a random forest for static letters and a long short-term memory network for letters with movement, and reports 99.1% and 66.0% on the authors' own data [@2026_jovlieva_webcam_uzbek_fingerspelling]; others classify Latin-script letters from finger-joint angles [@2024_kayumov_uzsl_latin_angular_signs] or detect letters with YOLO [@2024_akhmedov_uzsl_dactyl_yolo]. For isolated dynamic signs, a CNN–LSTM and a Squeezeformer-style Transformer were compared on a closed set of more than 500 classes with a random 70/15/15 split (87% and 91%) [@2026_ergashev_cnnlstm_vs_squeezeformer_uzsl], and an open-source pipeline recognises 50 signs of 10 signers with a 1D-CNN–Transformer hybrid, splitting the repetitions of all signers at random (about 87%) [@2025_akkomron_uzslr_isolated_dynamic]. No UzSL dataset is public, no published result was obtained on signers absent from training, and transfer from RSL has not been studied.
