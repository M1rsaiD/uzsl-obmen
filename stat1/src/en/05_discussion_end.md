# Discussion

**What the first signer-independent numbers for UzSL mean.** A new signer's isolated sign is recognised correctly in about a third of the cases and is among the five candidates in about half, on a vocabulary of 682 classes learned from a median of four recordings per class. These numbers cannot be compared with the 87–99% reported for UzSL so far: those were measured on signers present in training, on closed sets of 50 or fewer classes or of unknown composition, and the pilot experiment of Section 6.6 shows what such a measurement is worth (a median of 45 points of optimism). The LOSO estimate is confirmed by two external sources, so it is a fair description of the current state, and it defines the task for the next generation of UzSL recognisers: not higher accuracy on seen signers, but higher accuracy on unseen ones.

**Pretraining on a related sign language is the main lever.** Across the corpus (+14.7 points, every signer), the two external sources (+9.2 and +14.7 points), the pilot (18 of 18 comparisons) and the Bukva simulation (+59.7 points with one recording per class), pretraining on RSL is the one intervention that always helps, and it helps most when target data are scarcest. It also helps fingerspelling, but with only three signers the size of that gain is too uncertain to compare with words. These results support the hypothesis that RSL representations transfer to UzSL; they do not establish a linguistic relation between the languages, and a third, unrelated source (ASL) adds nothing that the data can establish.

**Architecture matters less than released baselines suggest.** On Slovo, on the corpus and on the pilot as a frozen encoder, the released SL-GCN is 2–4 points more accurate than our encoder; MPE is a cheaper model, not a more accurate one, and its value lies in the accuracy it reaches at 0.76 GFLOPs (0.196 GFLOPs without the graph), in the analysis of which components matter, and in transfer, where after full fine-tuning it is not inferior. The ablations point to one structural element, the grouping of keypoints into body-part tokens with their own coordinate frames; the skeletal graph can be replaced by a per-part MLP without loss. The same tokens are neutral on a large ASL dataset, so their benefit is tied to the regime of few recordings per class and hand-dominated signs. More important for the field is the 4–9-point gap between our re-implementations of ST-GCN and SL-GCN and their released code: a comparison with re-implemented baselines would have reversed the conclusion of this paper. Baselines should be run from released code wherever it exists, and re-implementations should be labelled as such.

**Evaluation practice.** Two measurements show how strongly signer overlap affects reported accuracy: the 45-point median gap between validation on the training participants and the test on a new one, and the two thirds of the official Slovo test set that come from training signers. Fixing budgets on a simulation with many signers and evaluating the last checkpoint, as done here for the pilot and the corpus, is a practical substitute for a validation set when the target language has only a few signers.

**Deployment.** In operations MPE is 2.6–2.9 times cheaper than the graph networks at the same window and 12.8 times cheaper than SL-GCN at its own test window, and its TensorFlow Lite export runs in 24 ms (6.6 ms without the graph) on a desktop CPU; in ONNX Runtime it is faster than both graph networks (10.8 against 15.0 and 25.6 ms on one thread), but in eager PyTorch it is not faster than ST-GCN, and no measurement on a phone exists yet. Floating-point operations are an incomplete proxy for latency, and the runtime changes the ranking; the final model must be measured on the device and runtime it will run on.

**Limitations.** The corpus is built from lessons: each lesson presenter has one studio and camera, so LOSO measures a new person together with new recording conditions, and the three largest vocabularies belong to three presenters. The automatic labels were verified against a dictionary for 113 recordings by one reviewer; the accuracy on words absent from the dictionary rests on the reviewer's judgement, and the planned second sample of lesson recordings and a second reviewer are still pending. Synonyms and Latin/Cyrillic duplicates are separate classes, which lowers accuracy with the full head. The corpus cannot be released, because the lesson videos and the keypoints derived from them identify the presenters; the code, fold lists and the construction procedure are released instead. The pilot has two main participants recorded in one session. The released ST-GCN and SL-GCN and MPE-A1 with the SL-GCN recipe were run with one seed on SD-5, and the keypoint Transformer and the simple baselines have no released counterparts. Ablations, the capacity study and the robustness tests were carried out on RSL data only; the robustness test uses independent synthetic noise, which only approximates the temporally correlated errors of a real keypoint detector, and a change of keypoint extractor was not tested.

# Conclusion

This study gives the first results on Uzbek Sign Language recognition that were obtained on signers absent from training, and it shows what determines them. A multi-signer corpus built from open video lessons with labels verified against a dictionary makes signer-disjoint evaluation possible; on it, a compact keypoint encoder recognises a new signer's sign with 32.0% top-1 and 52.0% top-5 accuracy on 682 classes, an estimate reproduced on two external sources. Pretraining on two public Russian Sign Language datasets raises accuracy for every held-out signer, by 14.7 points on the corpus and by 23 points on the pilot, and is the main lever available to a low-resource sign language. On the Russian datasets, under a signer-disjoint protocol with pre-registered decision rules, the released graph networks are 2–4 points more accurate than the encoder at 2.6–2.9 times the computation, while re-implementations of the same networks understate them by 4–9 points; body-part tokens, not the skeletal graph, carry the encoder's accuracy, and the graph can be removed to cut computation 3.9 times. Finally, validation on recordings of the training participants overestimates accuracy on a new signer by a median of 45 points. For low-resource sign languages, how a system is evaluated, what it is pretrained on and whether its baselines are run from released code matter more than its architecture. The next steps are a second reviewer and a larger reference sample for the labels, synonym merging verified by a native signer, more signers through the same construction procedure, recognition with a rejection option for a new signer, and latency measured on phones.
